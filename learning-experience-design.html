Learning Experience Design

A practical toolkit for moving from content to capability: diagnose the business need, identify the performance gap, design job-relevant learning, build practice and support, and measure whether behaviour changed.

From SME knowledge to workplace capability.
Capability Design View

Learning design starts before the course

The real question is not “What content do we have?” It is “What should people be able to do differently, under real workplace conditions, with confidence and support?”

Business Need→ Capability Gap→ Design→ Build→ Review→ Measure

1. Start with the performance question

Before designing a course, ask whether the problem is really a learning problem. The strongest learning experiences start with a clear business need, performance gap, audience understanding and task analysis.

Analyze

Performance Gap Analysis

What should people be doing? What are they doing today? What is stopping performance: knowledge, skill, workflow, tools, manager support, confidence or incentives?

Understand

Audience Analysis

Who are the learners? What do they already know? Are they new, experienced, resistant, overloaded, distributed, client-facing or manager-led?

Break down work

Task Analysis

Break the job into tasks, decisions, tools, inputs, outputs, risks, errors and moments where learners need support.

2. Learning objectives that are useful

A strong objective is not “understand the topic.” It states what the learner will do, under what condition, and how well it must be done.

PerformanceWhat should the learner be able to do?
ConditionUnder what situation, tool, case or constraint?
CriteriaHow well, how accurately, how quickly or to what standard?
ExampleGiven SME notes, create measurable objectives aligned to the required workplace behaviour.

3. Bloom's Taxonomy: choose the right learning demand

Use Bloom's Taxonomy to move from lower-order knowledge to higher-order capability. This helps align objectives, activities and assessments.

RememberRecall, list, identify.
UnderstandExplain, summarize, describe.
ApplyUse, perform, demonstrate.
AnalyzeDifferentiate, diagnose, compare.
EvaluateJudge, critique, recommend.
CreateDesign, build, produce.

4. Course structure rules

General → Specific

Start with the big picture

Show the overall process, context and relationships before teaching individual parts.

Chronological

Teach procedures in workflow order

For process content, sequence the learning in the order people perform the task in real work.

Cause → Effect

Teach concepts through consequences

For non-procedural content, explain the concept and then show its effect or application.

Overview module

Use contextual knowledge wisely

Place broad contextual knowledge in an overview module, then build job tasks and practice around terminal objectives.

5. Learning theories and design implications

Behaviorism

Useful for reinforcement, practice, compliance, SOPs and clear right/wrong responses.

Cognitivism

Useful for process learning, problem solving, mental models, decision-making and connecting prior knowledge to new information.

Constructivism

Useful for leadership, coaching, reflection, ambiguity, collaboration and learner-generated meaning.

6. Task-based learning strategy

Task-based learning focuses on doing the work, not just receiving content. Use the instruction map below to move from introduction to practice and assessment.

Intro→ Preparation→ Activity→ Summary→ Test
New learnersUse Show Me → Try It.
Experienced learnersUse discussion, multiple perspectives and practice.
Complex skillsUse realistic scenarios and feedback.
Performance supportAdd checklists, job aids, prompts and manager nudges.

7. ARCS model: design for motivation

John Keller's ARCS model helps designers plan for learner motivation through Attention, Relevance, Confidence and Satisfaction.

A

Attention

Use stories, scenarios, surprising facts, relatable problems or a question that makes the learner think.

R

Relevance

Connect the learning to the learner's work, prior experience, role expectations and real decision points.

C

Confidence

Build confidence through examples, guided practice, achievable challenges, feedback and clear success criteria.

S

Satisfaction

Create satisfaction through application, visible progress, job aids, recognition and workplace transfer.

8. Kirkpatrick evaluation model

Use Kirkpatrick to avoid measuring only completion. Plan evidence of reaction, learning, behaviour and results.

Level 1: ReactionWas the experience relevant, engaging and useful?
Level 2: LearningDid learners gain knowledge, skill, confidence or commitment?
Level 3: BehaviourAre learners applying the behaviour in the workplace?
Level 4: ResultsDid the initiative contribute to targeted outcomes?

9. DDI Success Profile lens

Use this when defining capability for a role, project or talent intervention. It is especially useful when you need to connect learning design to role success.

KnowledgeWhat people need to know.
ExperienceWhat people have done or need exposure to.
CompetenciesWhat people need to be able to do.
Personal attributesPatterns, preferences and attributes that influence performance.
